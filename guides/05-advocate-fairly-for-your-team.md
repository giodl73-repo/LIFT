# Advocate Fairly for Your Team

Advocacy moves accurate credit, context, resources, opportunity, and
relationships through the organization.

It is not secret scoring, favoritism, or saying every person's name in every
room.

## Use the advocacy sequence

```text
See
  -> Name
  -> Connect
  -> Open
  -> Back
  -> Transfer
```

### See

Understand the contribution and the conditions that made it possible.

### Name

Attribute the work accurately. Include shared ownership and enabling work.

### Connect

Connect the evidence to a legitimate decision, need, or organizational
priority.

### Open

Create fair access to relevant context, work, relationships, or forums.

### Back

Support the evidence when it is questioned, distorted, or omitted.

### Transfer

Build durable pathways so recognition and opportunity do not depend on one
manager's memory or presence.

## Run a fair-opportunity review

Before assigning a visible or developmental opportunity, ask:

- What capability or interest is relevant?
- Who knows the opportunity exists?
- Who can safely express interest?
- What access prerequisites are structural rather than merit-based?
- What support would make participation possible?
- How will contribution be attributed?
- Who has repeatedly received or missed similar access?
- What decision rule can be explained?

Do not choose people from a private LIFT score.

Do not concentrate advocacy on a small group merely because they appear closest
to a promotion or other employment decision.

## Build the pathway

For one person or piece of work:

```text
Evidence:
Audience with legitimate need:
Current route:
Likely distortion or delay:
Access needed:
Manager action:
Person's consent or preference:
Review trigger:
```

The person may not want more exposure, public credit, or a particular
opportunity. Ask rather than assume.

## Advocacy examples

- correct an attribution error;
- include the domain owner in the relevant decision;
- explain the person's contribution with specific evidence;
- make opportunity criteria visible;
- provide context and resources before a stretch assignment;
- protect appropriate credit during disagreement;
- transfer a relationship instead of remaining the permanent relay.

## What does not count

- generic praise;
- assigning more work without authority or support;
- exposing someone to senior audiences without preparation or consent;
- speaking for a person when they can speak for themselves;
- hiding opportunity criteria;
- using formal documentation to compensate for absent daily practice;
- comparing reports through M/S/E.

## Common traps

- visibility theater;
- favoritism disguised as sponsorship;
- over-crediting a visible presenter;
- erasing collaborative work;
- repeatedly relying on one senior relationship;
- assuming equal opportunity because the invitation was technically open;
- treating advocacy as a scarce prize to allocate by secret ranking.

Use the [pathway experiment](../toolkit/templates/pathway-experiment.md).

## What this guide cannot tell you

- who deserves promotion or reward;
- how many people a manager should champion;
- whether exposure is safe for a person;
- whether the organization will act on the evidence;
- whether equal treatment creates equitable access.
