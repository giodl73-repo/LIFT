# Using Stories and Metaphors in LIFT

This page uses optional stories to illustrate organizational conditions.

They do not prove what happened, reveal another person's intent, or replace the
evidence record.

Use this sequence:

```text
direct LIFT rule
  -> optional story
  -> useful transfer
  -> safe next action
  -> evidence boundary
  -> misuse warning
  -> stop condition
  -> plain-language fallback
```

The direct rule remains authoritative. If the story creates more certainty than
the evidence supports, stop using it.

## Choose a card

| If the situation is... | Use |
|---|---|
| conflict may rest on incomplete facts or ownership assumptions | [Check the model before assigning intent](#1-check-the-model-before-assigning-intent) |
| people are being asked for input before a decision | [Put the options on one surface](#2-put-the-options-on-one-surface) |
| the formal hierarchy does not explain an evidence or decision path | [The org chart is not the contact map](#3-the-org-chart-is-not-the-contact-map) |
| a manager, role, team, or organization is changing | [Name what carries and what requires current evidence](#4-name-what-carries-and-what-requires-current-evidence) |

The intended audience is a person using LIFT for private reflection, a
disclosed work conversation, or a legitimate process review. No source scene is
required; use the plain-language fallback when the story is unfamiliar,
unhelpful, or unsafe.

## Story contract

Every LIFT story or metaphor should:

- clarify one target situation;
- transfer only named relationships;
- preserve agency, power, and decision rights;
- lead to one bounded action;
- state what evidence is still needed;
- name how the story could mislead;
- say when the story stops applying;
- remain understandable without the metaphor.

Do not:

- label a person or team with a story pattern;
- treat vividness as evidence;
- make people obstacles, cargo, defects, or terrain;
- use empathy to erase established harm;
- use a metaphor to hide who owns a decision;
- use a story to pressure agreement;
- imply that a familiar source scene is culturally universal.

## 1. Check the model before assigning intent

### Direct rule

When facts are incomplete, check the shared facts and ownership assumptions
before assigning intent or blame.

### Illustrative story

Two people take food from one visible packet. One assumes the packet is theirs
and interprets the other person's behavior as taking without permission.

Later, they find their own unopened packet elsewhere.

The tension was real. The first ownership explanation was wrong.

### Useful transfer

| Story element | Organizational situation |
|---|---|
| visible packet | contested resource, responsibility, credit, or boundary |
| assumed ownership | an unverified model |
| another person's action | observable behavior whose intent remains unknown |
| unopened packet | disconfirming evidence |

### Safe next action

```text
pause accusation
  -> inspect shared facts and ownership
  -> ask what each party observed, when safe
  -> separate impact from intent
  -> choose clarification or repair
```

### Evidence boundary

The story does not prove that nobody acted badly. Misunderstanding is one
hypothesis among several.

Check:

- actual ownership and decision rights;
- observed behavior and impact;
- repeated conduct;
- power and safety conditions;
- prior clarification;
- counterevidence.

Do not require direct contact when there is a safety, retaliation, or power
concern.

### Misuse warning

Do not use this story to minimize established harm, excuse repeated behavior,
or pressure an affected person to accept shared blame.

### Stop condition

Once the material facts establish harm, duty, or a repeated behavior pattern,
stop using the story. Name accountability, repair, and the appropriate formal
or protected route.

### Plain-language fallback

> We do not know intent yet. Check the facts and ownership model before
> assigning blame.

## 2. Put the options on one surface

### Direct rule

Participation is more credible when real alternatives, criteria, constraints,
and decision authority are visible before commitment.

### Illustrative story

A team is asked to support a proposed change. Instead of presenting one answer
and collecting reactions, the decision owner places several feasible options
on one shared surface.

The group can see:

- what each option protects;
- what each option costs;
- which constraints are fixed;
- what input can still change;
- who will make the decision.

### Useful transfer

| Story element | Organizational situation |
|---|---|
| shared surface | inspectable decision record |
| options side by side | real alternatives |
| labels and constraints | criteria, costs, and boundaries |
| person making the final mark | decision authority |

### Safe next action

Record:

```text
Decision:
Real options:
Criteria:
Constraints:
Decision owner:
What input can still change:
Dissent or correction path:
```

### Evidence boundary

Visible options do not prove that participation is meaningful. Check whether:

- the alternatives are real;
- relevant information is available;
- affected people can safely respond;
- input arrives before the decision is fixed;
- the owner explains the disposition.

### Misuse warning

A shared board can perform inclusion while hiding a predetermined decision.

### Stop condition

If no material input can change the decision, stop calling the activity
participation. Explain the decision, rationale, remaining choices, and
correction path directly.

### Plain-language fallback

> Show the real options, criteria, constraints, and decision owner before
> asking people to commit.

## 3. The org chart is not the contact map

### Direct rule

Formal reporting lines do not reveal who actually receives context, sees
evidence, controls resources, or can correct a distorted account.

### Illustrative story

An organization chart shows a simple route from a team to a senior leader.

The work itself travels differently:

- one partner supplies critical context;
- another group controls a dependency;
- a project lead presents the result;
- the team's manager owns the formal escalation;
- the person who did the work is absent when tradeoffs are discussed.

The chart is accurate, but incomplete for the question being asked.

### Useful transfer

| Map element | Organizational situation |
|---|---|
| formal line | reporting and accountability |
| actual route | context, evidence, resource, or decision pathway |
| missing node | absent voice or unavailable owner |
| single bridge | concentrated dependency |

### Safe next action

For one legitimate work channel, record:

```text
Needed movement:
Formal route:
Observed route:
Decision owner:
Where meaning may be lost:
Which roles or groups repeatedly have or lack necessary access:
Purpose and deletion event:
Safe fallback:
```

### Evidence boundary

Use observed work interactions, decisions, and authorized records. Do not infer
private alliances or hidden influence from social proximity.

### Misuse warning

The contact map can become surveillance, a covert influence graph, or a shadow
personnel record.

### Stop condition

Record roles or affected groups rather than unnecessary person-level
relationships. Stop collecting when the named work purpose is satisfied.
Delete detail that is not necessary for the pathway decision.

### Plain-language fallback

> Map how this specific evidence or decision actually travels, not who appears
> socially connected.

## 4. Name what carries and what requires current evidence

### Direct rule

A transition can carry work evidence while changing the people, authority,
context, and advocacy paths that make the evidence usable.

### Illustrative story

A person moves with several bags.

Some items are already packed and remain useful. Some need translation for the
destination. Some cannot travel. Other necessities must be obtained after
arrival.

The move does not erase what existed before. It changes what is immediately
available in the new setting.

### Useful transfer

| Story element | Transition evidence |
|---|---|
| packed item | portable skill, artifact, or work evidence |
| translated item | evidence requiring new context |
| item left behind | authority or relationship tied to the prior setting |
| item obtained after arrival | current trust, access, or operating contract |

### Safe next action

For each category, choose `carries`, `partly carries`, `requires current
evidence`, `not portable`, or `unknown`:

```text
skills:
artifacts:
work evidence:
context:
trust evidence:
work relationships relevant to the role:
authority:
commitments:
```

Preserve source, date, and limits.

### Evidence boundary

Do not assume that all trust, sponsorship, reputation, or authority resets.
Check what the destination already knows, recognizes, supports, or requires.

### Misuse warning

Rebuilding cost is not evidence that a person should remain in an unsafe,
hostile, or unsuitable environment.

### Stop condition

Retire the transition metaphor once current evidence and operating contracts
exist. Continue using the present-state record rather than narrating the person
as permanently new.

### Plain-language fallback

> List which evidence remains applicable, which needs context, and which
> relationships or authority must be established again.

## Relationship to FRAMES

This page adapts the controlled-frame method developed in
[FRAMES](https://github.com/giodl73-repo/FRAMES).

FRAMES owns the general method for source scenes, transfer maps, action cues,
evidence boundaries, misuse warnings, audience transfer, and frame lifecycle.

LIFT owns the M/S/E model, evidence rules, ethical boundaries, module meaning,
and public disposition of these examples.

The stories do not add a score, pattern identity, prediction, or employment
decision rule.
