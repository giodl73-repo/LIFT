# Module 08 Public Sources

These sources support adjacent concepts. They do not validate LIFT transition
statuses, automatic score resets, inherited M/S/E, fixed transition windows, or
career predictions.

## Newcomer adjustment

- Bauer, T. N., Bodner, T., Erdogan, B., Truxillo, D. M., and Tucker, J. S.
  (2007), "Newcomer Adjustment During Organizational Socialization: A
  Meta-Analytic Review of Antecedents, Outcomes, and Methods," *Journal of
  Applied Psychology*, 92(3), 707-721.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.3.707).

  The meta-analysis identifies role clarity, self-efficacy, and social
  acceptance as central adjustment indicators related to work outcomes. The
  studies primarily concern organizational entry, so transfer to every LIFT
  transition type is limited.

- Bauer, T. N., and Green, S. G. (1998), "Testing the Combined Effects of
  Newcomer Information Seeking and Manager Behavior on Socialization,"
  *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 83(1), 72-83.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.83.1.72).

  This longitudinal study reports distinct contributions from manager behavior
  and newcomer adjustment processes. It does not support placing the entire
  transition burden on the newcomer.

## Work-role transitions

- Nicholson, N. (1984), "A Theory of Work Role Transitions,"
  *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 29(2), 172-191.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2393172).

  This theoretical article distinguishes personal adjustment from attempts to
  shape a new role. It is conceptual precedent, not validation of the LIFT
  loop.

## Internal mobility

- Vantaggiato, F. P., Murdoch, Z., Kassim, H., Geys, B., and Connolly, S.
  (2024), "Intraorganizational Mobility and Employees' Work-Related Contact
  Patterns: Evidence from Panel Data in the European Commission," *Journal of
  Public Administration Research and Theory*, 34(4), 598-610.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/muae014).

  The study reports that internal moves can change the breadth and depth of
  work contacts. Its single institutional context limits generalization.

## Returning employees

- Arnold, J. D., Van Iddekinge, C. H., Campion, M. C., Bauer, T. N., and
  Campion, M. A. (2021), "Welcome Back? Job Performance and Turnover of
  Boomerang Employees Compared to Internal and External Hires," *Journal of
  Management*, 47(8), 2198-2225.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206320936335).

  In a large longitudinal management sample, returning employees performed
  similarly to comparison hires initially, improved less over time, and had
  higher subsequent turnover. The findings challenge a universal return
  advantage.

- Keller, J. R., Kehoe, R. R., Bidwell, M., Collings, D., and Myer, A. (2021),
  "In With the Old? Examining When Boomerang Employees Outperform New Hires,"
  *Academy of Management Journal*, 64(6), 1654-1684.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2019.1340).

  This study finds context-dependent advantages for returning employees,
  especially where internal coordination matters. Together with other
  findings, it supports current diligence rather than a universal premium.

## Return to work

- Figueredo, J.-M., Garcia-Ael, C., Gragnano, A., and Topa, G. (2020),
  "Well-Being at Work after Return to Work: A Systematic Review,"
  *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 17(20),
  7490.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17207490).

  The review identifies individual, job, and organizational factors associated
  with well-being after return to work. Much of the underlying literature is
  health-related and should not be generalized to every form of leave.
