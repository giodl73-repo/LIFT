# Module 05: Trust and Team Health

Status: draft

## Purpose

This module helps teams and leaders hold two truths together:

1. a manager may have strong upward support;
2. people on the team may experience unclear, unsafe, unfair, or unsustainable
   management conditions.

Neither truth automatically erases the other.

Team health is:

> the team's current ability to coordinate, speak up, learn, carry sustainable
> work, and receive fair support.

It is not a popularity score, happiness index, promotion gate, or total measure
of manager quality.

## Three separate records

Do not compress team health into M/S/E.

```text
management acts
team experience
operating context and outcomes
```

### 1. Management acts

Observable acts may include:

- setting or clarifying priorities;
- defining decision rights;
- giving usable feedback;
- allocating workload and opportunity;
- responding to disagreement or mistakes;
- developing people;
- representing evidence and resource needs;
- correcting unfairness or misattribution.

An act is not automatically effective because it occurred.

### 2. Team experience

Team members may experience:

- clarity or confusion;
- safety or interpersonal risk;
- usable support or abandonment;
- fair or concentrated opportunity;
- sustainable or damaging workload;
- consistent or arbitrary accountability;
- accurate or distorted credit.

Experience can vary within the same team. An average can hide a subgroup with a
materially different experience.

### 3. Operating context and outcomes

Relevant context and outcomes may include:

- staffing and skill mix;
- inherited systems and commitments;
- delivery and reliability;
- workload and interruptions;
- resource decisions;
- organizational change;
- absence, leave, transfer, and turnover;
- customer or stakeholder conditions.

An outcome may have multiple causes. A functioning team can miss a target. A
team can deliver while experiencing harmful management conditions.

## Five team-health lenses

### Direction

Can people explain:

- what matters now;
- what success and acceptable tradeoffs mean;
- who decides;
- what changed and why?

Clarity is not certainty. A manager can be clear about unresolved questions.

### Voice and learning

Can people:

- raise risks, mistakes, dissent, and uncertainty;
- ask for help;
- challenge a decision without retaliation;
- learn from failure without erasing accountability?

Psychological safety is not comfort, agreement, or protection from standards.

### Work and sustainability

Can the team:

- carry the expected workload with available resources;
- recover from ordinary disruption;
- stop or renegotiate work when constraints change;
- operate without recurring heroics?

Individual resilience should not be used to conceal a structurally damaging
workload.

### Fairness and inclusion

Are:

- credit and accountability attributed accurately;
- opportunities allocated through relevant criteria;
- standards applied consistently;
- location, schedule, identity, role, or relationship access creating repeated
  disadvantage;
- correction and appeal routes usable?

Identical treatment is not always fair treatment. Different treatment still
requires a legitimate, inspectable reason.

### Growth and support

Do people receive:

- expectations before evaluation;
- task-relevant feedback;
- appropriate autonomy and support;
- meaningful development discussion;
- fair access to learning and sponsorship;
- honest limits rather than unsupported promises?

Growth is broader than promotion.

## Trust

Trust involves willingness to accept vulnerability based on expectations about
another person's ability, care, and integrity.

Trust is not equivalent to:

- liking the manager;
- agreeing with every decision;
- staying in the organization;
- avoiding conflict;
- giving a high survey rating;
- praising the manager to senior leaders.

Trust may be domain-specific. A person may trust a manager to represent their
work accurately but not to handle a sensitive concern, or the reverse.

## The reconciliation table

Use this table to choose inquiry, not a verdict.

| Upward support | Team experience | Initial response |
|---|---|---|
| strong | broadly healthy | Preserve evidence and continue checking for hidden subgroup differences |
| strong | recurring concerns | Protect feedback, investigate specifics, and do not let reputation pre-decide the result |
| weak or unknown | broadly healthy | Improve the evidence pathway without asking the team to campaign for the manager |
| weak or unknown | recurring concerns | Provide support and accountability while identifying inherited and structural conditions |
| any | mixed across groups | Examine who experiences what, where, and under which conditions |

Upward support describes advocacy. It is not certification of management
quality.

Team experience describes lived conditions. It is not an automatic judgment of
intent or sole causality.

## Safe evidence sources

### Direct observation

Examples:

- a decision and its rationale;
- a meeting response to dissent;
- allocation of ownership or opportunity;
- a feedback exchange;
- workload and escalation behavior.

Direct observation is bounded to what was actually seen.

### Work artifacts

Examples:

- goals and decision records;
- ownership maps;
- workload plans;
- feedback and development commitments;
- incident reviews;
- opportunity criteria.

Artifacts can reveal process but not every person's experience.

### Confidential themes

Recurring themes from several people may reveal a pattern.

Safeguards:

- state the purpose before collecting feedback;
- separate themes from identifying details;
- do not promise anonymity that cannot be protected;
- do not pressure people to provide positive or negative testimony;
- provide a formal route for serious misconduct;
- give the manager actionable themes without inviting identity hunting.

### Aggregate surveys

Surveys can reveal trends and questions. They do not directly measure truth.

Inspect:

- response rate and missingness;
- team size and re-identification risk;
- wording and construct quality;
- subgroup differences;
- recent events;
- change over time;
- whether low safety may suppress response.

Do not treat a benchmark difference as a manager verdict.

### Operating patterns

Examples:

- recurring crisis work;
- decision delay;
- error concealment;
- uneven opportunity;
- transfers or departures;
- delivery instability.

Patterns require plausible alternative explanations and contextual evidence.

## The signal interpretation record

```text
Signal:
Evidence source:
People and period represented:
Specific observation or theme:
Plausible explanations:
Corroborating evidence:
Counterevidence:
Privacy or retaliation risk:
Responsible owner:
Safe next action:
Recheck date:
```

This record is not a vote count. Several repeated weak signals do not
automatically become proof, and one serious report should not be dismissed
because it is not repeated.

## Survey and small-team privacy

In a small team, demographic cuts, quoted comments, timing, role details, or
writing style can reveal a respondent.

Do not:

- publish identifiable comments;
- ask the manager to guess who said what;
- combine confidential feedback with performance retaliation;
- report a subgroup too small to protect;
- imply anonymity when administrators can access identities;
- collect sensitive data without a defined use and retention policy.

If privacy cannot be protected, use a qualified confidential channel rather
than pretending aggregation solves the problem.

## Skip-level and next-level conversations

A next-level leader may need direct evidence about team conditions.

Safe practice:

- explain the purpose and boundaries;
- avoid turning the conversation into a secret case-building exercise;
- ask about work conditions and observable behavior;
- provide a route for protected concerns;
- summarize actionable themes without unnecessary attribution;
- tell the manager how themes will be used;
- follow through visibly enough that speaking up is not futile.

Team members should never be expected to advocate upward for their manager as a
condition of support or opportunity.

## Attrition, transfer, and absence

A departure can be important evidence. It is not a self-interpreting verdict.

Possible contributors include:

- manager relationship;
- role fit;
- workload;
- compensation;
- organizational direction;
- personal circumstances;
- location or schedule;
- external opportunity;
- discrimination or exclusion;
- cumulative factors.

Inspect patterns, stated reasons, context, and contradictory evidence. Respect
privacy. Do not declare the manager either responsible or irrelevant from a
single departure.

## Contradictions and serious concerns

Ordinary development feedback may be addressed through coaching, role design,
and follow-up.

Potential harassment, discrimination, retaliation, threats, fraud, or other
serious misconduct requires an appropriate formal process. Do not reduce it to
a team-health theme or ask the manager to investigate themselves.

When evidence conflicts:

1. protect people and records;
2. separate allegation, observation, inference, and outcome;
3. assess immediacy and severity;
4. use an independent process where required;
5. let the manager respond when safe and appropriate;
6. avoid premature public conclusions;
7. record uncertainty and next decisions.

## Fairness to the manager

Accountability and procedural fairness can coexist.

A manager should ordinarily receive:

- behaviorally specific, actionable themes;
- relevant context and expected standard;
- an opportunity to provide evidence or correction;
- support proportionate to a development issue;
- a clear formal process for serious allegations;
- protection from rumor being presented as established fact.

Fairness does not require exposing confidential sources or delaying immediate
safety action.

## M/S/E interpretation

M/S/E remains an advocacy profile.

```text
M: Does the manager's direct manager understand and advocate for the manager's
   actual management work?

S: Does the next level have accurate evidence about management acts, team
   experience, operating context, and learning?

E: Does senior awareness, advocacy, or sponsorship rest on relevant evidence
   rather than reputation, title, or team output alone?
```

Do not:

- create a second M score from team feedback;
- multiply M by a survey or trust factor;
- average team health into M/S/E;
- use team members as compelled advocates;
- let M/S/E replace a fair management review.

## Organization responsibilities

Organizations should:

- create protected feedback and appeal routes;
- train leaders to interpret survey and qualitative evidence;
- protect small-group privacy;
- investigate serious concerns independently;
- separate development feedback from retaliation;
- provide managers with support and actionable expectations;
- inspect structural workload, staffing, and opportunity patterns;
- prevent senior sponsorship from overriding credible harm evidence;
- prevent anonymous rumor from becoming unreviewable fact;
- communicate what changed after feedback when privacy allows.

## What this module does not claim

- Team health is a single score.
- Trust means liking, agreement, or retention.
- Silence proves safety or dissatisfaction.
- One departure proves manager failure.
- High delivery proves healthy management.
- A survey measures manager quality directly.
- Team feedback should automatically override every other form of evidence.
- Upward advocacy validates management effectiveness.
- Anonymous comments are always safe or accurate.
- Every contradiction can be resolved.
- Team members should manage their manager's career.

Public sources support adjacent claims about psychological safety, voice and
silence, trust, turnover, and multisource feedback. They do not validate a
hidden team-trust score, an automatic promotion rule, or single-signal
diagnosis.
