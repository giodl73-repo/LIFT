# Module 05 Public Sources

These sources support adjacent concepts. They do not validate the LIFT
three-record model, a hidden team-trust score, an automatic promotion rule, or
single-signal diagnosis.

## Psychological safety, voice, and silence

- Edmondson, A. (1999), "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work
  Teams," *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 44(2), 350-383.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999).

  This study introduced team psychological safety as a shared belief about
  interpersonal risk and connected it with learning behavior. The study used
  51 teams in one company, so setting and causal limits matter.

- Frazier, M. L., Fainshmidt, S., Klinger, R. L., Pezeshkan, A., and Vracheva,
  V. (2017), "Psychological Safety: A Meta-Analytic Review and Extension,"
  *Personnel Psychology*, 70(1), 113-165.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12183).

  The meta-analysis reports relationships among psychological safety,
  leadership support, learning, engagement, performance, and related outcomes.
  Much of the underlying evidence is self-reported or cross-sectional, and
  context and culture affect interpretation.

- Morrison, E. W. (2014), "Employee Voice and Silence," *Annual Review of
  Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*, 1, 173-197.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-031413-091328).

  This review describes voice and silence as context-dependent behavior shaped
  by factors including fear, futility, leadership, and climate. Absence of
  voice is therefore not a self-interpreting measure of safety.

## Trust in leadership

- Mayer, R. C., Davis, J. H., and Schoorman, F. D. (1995), "An Integrative
  Model of Organizational Trust," *Academy of Management Review*, 20(3),
  709-734.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335).

  The model defines trust through willingness to accept vulnerability and
  distinguishes ability, benevolence, and integrity. It is a theoretical model,
  not a team-health scoring instrument.

- Dirks, K. T., and Ferrin, D. L. (2002), "Trust in Leadership:
  Meta-Analytic Findings and Implications for Research and Practice,"
  *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 87(4), 611-628.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.87.4.611).

  This meta-analysis links trust in leadership with attitudes, behaviors, and
  performance-related outcomes. It does not make trust equivalent to
  popularity or prove that one leader causes every outcome.

## Turnover

- Rubenstein, A. L., Eberly, M. B., Lee, T. W., and Mitchell, T. R. (2018),
  "Surveying the Forest: A Meta-Analysis, Moderator Investigation, and
  Future-Oriented Discussion of the Antecedents of Voluntary Employee
  Turnover," *Personnel Psychology*, 71(1), 23-65.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12226).

  This meta-analysis examines many individual, job, and organizational
  antecedents and contextual moderators. LIFT uses it to reject single-cause
  interpretation of a departure.

## Multisource feedback

- Nowack, K. M., and Mashihi, S. (2012), "Evidence-Based Answers to 15
  Questions About Leveraging 360-Degree Feedback," *Consulting Psychology
  Journal: Practice and Research*, 64(3), 157-182.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030011).

  This review describes modest, implementation-dependent benefits and the need
  for follow-up and developmental framing. LIFT does not treat multisource
  feedback as a self-executing high-stakes verdict.

- Lockyer, J., and Sargeant, J. (2022), "Multisource Feedback: An Overview of
  Its Use and Application as a Formative Assessment," *Canadian Medical
  Education Journal*, 13(4), 30-35.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.36834/cmej.73775).

  This overview discusses formative use, aggregation, and confidentiality.
  Its medical-education context limits direct transfer, especially for small
  workplace teams.
