# Module 07: Choosing an Environment

Status: draft

## Purpose

This module helps a person inspect a prospective role, manager, team, and
organization without pretending that limited pre-join evidence predicts a
career.

The central practice is reciprocal diligence:

> the employer evaluates job-relevant candidate evidence, and the candidate
> receives enough specific and honest environment evidence to make a decision
> under uncertainty.

Pre-join M/S/E is `unknown` by default.

Reciprocal does not mean equal power. The employer normally controls more of
the process, information, and decision timing. That makes proactive
transparency an organizational responsibility, not a test of candidate
confidence or negotiation skill.

## Six environment lenses

### 1. Role reality

Inspect:

- the work that actually needs to be done;
- scope, outcomes, and decision authority;
- retained individual or management duties;
- workload, on-call, travel, location, and schedule expectations;
- dependencies and inherited problems;
- why the role is open;
- what may change after joining.

Questions:

```text
What would I own that I do not merely influence?
Which decisions can this role make without additional approval?
What work repeatedly consumes more time than expected?
Why is the role open now?
What changed in the role description during the last year?
```

A polished job description is a claim, not direct evidence.

### 2. Manager practice

Inspect observable management behavior rather than personality or reputation.

Questions:

```text
Can you share a recent decision you delegated and the guardrails you used?
What is an example of team feedback that changed how you managed?
How do you handle disagreement about priorities?
How do you represent shared contribution outside the team?
What does useful performance feedback look like here?
What support do you provide when an assignment stretches someone?
```

Warmth is useful information. It does not establish clarity, fairness,
advocacy, or follow-through.

### 3. Team conditions

Inspect:

- priority and decision clarity;
- workload and recurring crisis patterns;
- voice, disagreement, and learning;
- fairness of credit and opportunity;
- collaboration and handoffs;
- stability and recent change.

Questions:

```text
What commonly creates rework?
How does the team respond when someone raises a risk late?
Which work is hardest to staff or sustain?
How are visible assignments allocated?
What has the team stopped doing recently?
```

Do not ask a current employee to disclose another person's private performance
or personal circumstances.

### 4. Evidence pathways

Inspect whether work can become understood and represented beyond one person.

Questions:

```text
How does this role's work reach the next-level leader?
Can contributors present or decide directly within their scope?
How are cross-team contribution and shared credit preserved?
What happens to evidence when the manager or organization changes?
Can you give a recent example of someone gaining broader access through the
work itself?
```

These answers describe possible routes. They do not establish future M/S/E.

### 5. Opportunity and development

Inspect:

- how meaningful assignments are allocated;
- feedback and development support;
- promotion and role-change criteria;
- specialist and management paths;
- internal mobility;
- support for declining an opportunity.

Questions:

```text
What evidence is used to assign broader scope?
How are people considered when several are ready for one opportunity?
What development can the role provide even if promotion is unavailable?
Can someone move between management and specialist paths without stigma?
What opportunity is commonly described but rarely available?
```

Past promotions do not prove that the same path, criteria, or opportunity will
exist for a new person.

### 6. Stability, constraints, and terms

Inspect:

- reporting line and likely changes;
- funding and staffing assumptions;
- organizational growth, contraction, or reorganization;
- work-location policy;
- compensation and benefits;
- employment, visa, or contract constraints;
- offer conditions and decision timing.

Questions:

```text
Which parts of this role are funded or approved, and which are planned?
What organizational change is already known?
Who owns the decision if scope or reporting changes?
Which statements can be reflected in the written offer or role document?
What is the safest way to raise a material mismatch after joining?
```

This module is not legal or financial advice.

## Evidence quality before joining

| Evidence type | Example | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Direct observation | how interviewers handle disagreement or correction | one interaction may not represent ordinary work |
| Written term or artifact | offer, role scope, work policy, ownership document | documents may change and may not be contractual |
| Specific example | a recent delegation, feedback, or opportunity decision | selected examples can be unusually favorable |
| Corroborated pattern | similar specific accounts from several legitimate sources | shared scripts can create false consistency |
| General statement | "we value growth" | weak without behavior or example |
| Reputation or ambient signal | brand, review site, public profile | context, selection, and verification are limited |
| Inference | predicted manager quality or future sponsorship | preserve as `unknown`, not fact |

Use the strongest available evidence without pretending any pre-join source is
complete.

## Ask the same question across sources

When practical, ask a manager, peer, stakeholder, or next-level leader a
similar work-relevant question.

Example:

```text
How are priorities changed after work begins?
```

Compare:

- specificity;
- consistency;
- acknowledged tradeoffs;
- different role perspectives;
- what remains unknown.

Differences do not automatically prove deception. They may reveal role
boundaries, recent change, or genuine disagreement worth understanding.

Do not turn the process into covert interrogation.

## Public information boundary

Official policies, public filings, organizational announcements, and credible
reporting may identify useful questions.

Do not:

- build a personal dossier about a prospective manager or employee;
- infer protected or private characteristics;
- use personal social-media content as a hidden assessment;
- contact someone's personal network without agreement;
- treat an unverified online account as fact.

The aim is to understand work conditions, not investigate private lives.

## Reverse references

Candidates are often asked to provide references. Organizations can reciprocate
by providing legitimate access to environment evidence.

Possible sources:

- the hiring manager;
- a future peer;
- a cross-functional partner;
- the next-level leader;
- a person who recently changed roles within the team;
- a realistic work sample or team artifact with private information removed.

Participation should be part of an agreed process. Do not secretly contact
current or former employees for private information.

## Warm referrals and senior involvement

A warm referral may provide:

- more context;
- a trusted introduction;
- easier access to relevant people;
- evidence that someone is willing to recommend a conversation.

It does not guarantee:

- manager support after joining;
- next-level advocacy;
- senior sponsorship;
- promotion;
- portability of the referring relationship.

Senior involvement in hiring may create attention or expectations. Confirm:

- whether the hiring manager supports the role and scope;
- who owns day-to-day decisions;
- what commitment was actually made;
- whether the attention continues after hiring;
- which statements can be documented.

Do not convert entry path into an arrival M/S/E score.

## What not to infer

Do not infer manager quality, candidate quality, or future advocacy from:

- title or prestige;
- promotion speed;
- manager tenure;
- organization size;
- former-report job titles;
- public professional profiles;
- remote or hybrid work;
- career gaps;
- lateral or nonlinear careers;
- interview fluency;
- one employee review;
- one executive meeting.

These may generate questions. They do not answer them.

## The reciprocal diligence record

```text
Lens:
Question or decision need:
Evidence source:
Specific evidence:
Corroboration:
Counterevidence:
Unknowns:
Privacy or power concern:
Importance to me:
Possible mitigation:
Review trigger:
```

Do not use the record to secretly score people.

## Decide with requirements, not a magic total

Classify your decision criteria:

```text
Must be true:
Must not be true:
Strong preference:
Can learn after joining:
Unknown I can tolerate:
Unknown I cannot tolerate:
```

Then review:

- evidence strength;
- consequence if wrong;
- reversibility;
- financial and personal constraints;
- mitigation;
- decision deadline.

Do not average a severe non-negotiable with several attractive but unrelated
features.

## Minimum diligence when leverage is limited

Not every candidate can request additional interviews, delay a decision, or
negotiate from safety.

At minimum, try to confirm:

1. actual manager and reporting line;
2. core scope and decision authority;
3. work location, schedule, travel, and on-call expectations;
4. compensation and major conditions;
5. why the role is open;
6. how performance is understood;
7. known organizational change;
8. what is documented.

If asking is unsafe or impossible, record the gap as an environment unknown.
Do not treat limited candidate leverage as poor diligence.

## Warning signals

Material warning signals may include:

- basic role facts change repeatedly without acknowledgment;
- interviewers give incompatible accounts of ownership or workload;
- the organization refuses to identify the actual manager;
- expected location, travel, or on-call work appears late;
- development or promotion promises cannot be tied to criteria or authority;
- the candidate is pressured to decide before material questions can be
  answered;
- interview questions seek irrelevant protected or private information;
- no route exists to address a material mismatch after joining.

One vague answer is usually weak evidence, not proof of bad faith.

## Equity and access

Candidate diligence is shaped by:

- economic need;
- immigration or contract constraints;
- caregiving and schedule;
- disability and accessibility;
- location;
- professional networks;
- prior exclusion;
- negotiation norms;
- urgency and competing offers.

Organizations should not make transparency available only to candidates with
senior networks or high bargaining power.

Provide core role and environment evidence proactively, in accessible formats,
to every candidate for the same role.

Do not grade candidates on how many diligence questions they ask or how
confidently they negotiate.

Do not use "fit" to reward similarity, shared background, communication style,
or informal cultural comfort.

## For organizations: an environment evidence brief

Organizations can publish or provide:

```text
Role purpose and current priorities:
Manager and reporting line:
Decision authority:
Team structure and dependencies:
Work location, schedule, travel, and on-call:
Known constraints and inherited problems:
How feedback and performance are handled:
How opportunity and development are allocated:
How work reaches broader decision forums:
Recent organizational changes:
Expected changes:
Correction or mismatch route:
Document owner and date:
```

The brief is a realistic preview, not marketing copy or a guarantee that the
environment will not change.

## Prohibited employer use

LIFT must not be used to:

- infer a candidate's M/S/E from career history;
- rank candidates by presumed sponsorship;
- penalize career gaps, remote work, or nonlinear paths;
- scrape social media for private or protected information;
- classify candidates by former employer prestige;
- route opportunity using a covert manager or candidate score;
- replace structured, job-relevant selection.

Module 07 is for environment transparency and candidate agency, not candidate
profiling.

## After joining

Treat pre-join beliefs as hypotheses.

Review material conditions when:

- the manager or reporting line changes;
- role scope differs from the documented account;
- location or schedule requirements change;
- promised authority or resources do not exist;
- team or organizational conditions materially shift.

Record the evidence, ask for correction where safe, and decide whether to
adapt, renegotiate, transfer, or leave. Module 08 examines transitions in
depth.

## M/S/E interpretation

Before joining:

```text
M: unknown
S: unknown
E: unknown
```

Possible routes may be inspected, but advocacy evidence normally requires
observed behavior after a working relationship exists.

Do not assign scores to:

- a prospective manager;
- a future next-level relationship;
- a senior interviewer;
- a promised sponsor;
- an organization.

## What this module does not claim

- A prospective manager's M/S/E can be inferred reliably.
- Former-report promotions prove manager quality.
- A warm referral creates sponsorship.
- Senior interview involvement predicts advancement.
- Prestige predicts a healthy environment.
- Perceived fit proves future satisfaction or performance.
- A realistic preview removes uncertainty.
- Every candidate can conduct equal diligence.
- One inconsistent answer proves deception.
- Environment choice determines a person's outcome.

## External evidence boundary

Public sources support adjacent ideas about realistic job previews, applicant
attraction, fit, structured interviews, psychological contracts, and online
inference risk. They do not validate pre-join M/S/E inference, manager scoring,
entry-path destiny, or career-timing predictions.
