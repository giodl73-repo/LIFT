# Module 04 Public Sources

These sources support adjacent concepts. They do not validate the LIFT
four-pass transition, a fixed score reset, a behavioral threshold, or a
promotion timeline.

## First-time manager and identity transition

- Hill, L. A. (1992), *Becoming a Manager: Mastery of a New Identity*,
  Harvard Business School Press.
  [Publisher record](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=160).

  Hill's longitudinal study of new managers describes management as a profound
  role and identity transition learned substantially through experience. The
  original study is small and reflects its organizational and historical
  setting.

- Bolander, P., Holmberg, I., and Fellbom, E. (2019), "Learning to Become
  Manager: The Identity Work of First-Time Managers," *Management Learning*,
  50(3), 282-301.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1350507618820711).

  This longitudinal research presents first-time manager identity as iterative
  and socially situated. It does not establish a universal transition sequence
  or deadline.

- Ibarra, H. (1999), "Provisional Selves: Experimenting with Image and Identity
  in Professional Adaptation," *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 44(4),
  764-791.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2667055).

  The study describes professional identity adaptation through experimentation
  and evaluation of provisional selves. Its population and setting limit
  direct generalization to all new managers.

- DeRue, D. S., and Ashford, S. J. (2010), "Who Will Lead and Who Will Follow?
  A Social Process of Leadership Identity Construction in Organizations,"
  *Academy of Management Review*, 35(4), 627-647.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2010.53503267).

  This theoretical model treats leadership identity as socially claimed and
  granted rather than automatically created by formal position.

## Delegation and enabling conditions

- Lee, A., Willis, S., and Tian, A. W. (2018), "Empowering Leadership: A
  Meta-Analytic Examination of Incremental Contribution, Mediation, and
  Moderation," *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 39, 306-325.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.2220).

  The meta-analysis reports generally positive relationships with performance,
  citizenship behavior, and creativity through mechanisms including trust and
  psychological empowerment. Much underlying evidence is correlational and
  effects depend on context.

## Psychological safety

- Edmondson, A. C., and Bransby, D. P. (2023), "Psychological Safety Comes of
  Age: Observed Themes in an Established Literature," *Annual Review of
  Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*, 10, 55-78.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-120920-055217).

  This review synthesizes research connecting psychological safety with
  speaking up, learning, and related outcomes. Psychological safety is not the
  same as comfort, agreement, or absence of standards.

## Goals and feedback

- Locke, E. A., and Latham, G. P. (2002), "Building a Practically Useful
  Theory of Goal Setting and Task Motivation: A 35-Year Odyssey,"
  *American Psychologist*, 57(9), 705-717.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705).

  This review supports the importance of specific, challenging goals under
  suitable conditions. LIFT does not treat goal clarity as sufficient for good
  management.

- Kluger, A. N., and DeNisi, A. (1996), "The Effects of Feedback
  Interventions on Performance: A Historical Review, a Meta-Analysis, and a
  Preliminary Feedback Intervention Theory," *Psychological Bulletin*, 119(2),
  254-284.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254).

  This meta-analysis finds that feedback effects vary and can be negative,
  particularly when attention moves away from the task. LIFT therefore avoids
  treating feedback volume as evidence of effectiveness.
