# Module 09 Public Sources

These sources support adjacent concepts. They do not validate LIFT scores,
demographic correction formulas, individual bias diagnoses, or employment
decisions.

## Merit systems and unequal rewards

- Castilla, E. J., and Benard, S. (2010), "The Paradox of Meritocracy in
  Organizations," *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 55(4), 543-676.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2189/asqu.2010.55.4.543).

  Experimental studies found more favorable rewards for an equally qualified
  man in organizations presented as meritocratic. Formal merit language alone
  did not eliminate bias.

- Castilla, E. J. (2008), "Gender, Race, and Meritocracy in Organizational
  Careers," *American Journal of Sociology*, 113(6), 1479-1526.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/588738).

  This organizational study documents performance-reward differences after
  accounting for performance evaluations. It supports inspecting career
  returns rather than assuming a common rating produces an equitable outcome.

## Networks and access

- Ibarra, H. (1992), "Homophily and Differential Returns: Sex Differences in
  Network Structure and Access in an Advertising Firm," *Administrative
  Science Quarterly*, 37(3), 422-447.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2393451).

  The study reports differences in network structure and returns in one firm.
  It supports examining access and network mechanisms while limiting
  generalization beyond the study context.

## Voice, safety, and silence

- Edmondson, A. (1999), "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work
  Teams," *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 44(2), 350-383.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999).

  This study relates team psychological safety to learning behavior. LIFT uses
  it as adjacent support for treating voice conditions as part of evidence
  safety, not as proof that silence has one cause.

- Morrison, E. W. (2014), "Employee Voice and Silence," *Annual Review of
  Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*, 1(1), 173-197.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-031413-091328).

  This review examines why people speak up or remain silent, including expected
  efficacy, risk, and organizational response. Silence should not be treated as
  agreement or low commitment.

## Workplace privacy and monitoring

- Vitak, J., and Zimmer, M. (2023), "Surveillance and the Future of Work:
  Exploring Employees' Attitudes Toward Monitoring in a Post-COVID
  Workplace," *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*, 28(4), zmad007.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmad007).

  The study examines employee attitudes toward workplace monitoring and the
  importance of context, transparency, and perceived appropriateness.

- Ebert, I., Wildhaber, I., and Adams-Prassl, J. (2021), "Big Data in the
  Workplace: Privacy Due Diligence as a Human Rights-Based Approach to Employee
  Privacy Protection," *Big Data & Society*, 8(1).
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517211013051).

  The article proposes a due-diligence approach to workplace data processing
  that considers purpose, necessity, proportionality, risk, and accountability.

- International Labour Organization (1997), *Protection of Workers' Personal
  Data: An ILO Code of Practice*.
  [Official publication](https://www.ilo.org/publications/protection-workers%E2%80%99-personal-data-ilo-code-practice).

  This nonbinding code provides authoritative guidance on collection, use,
  storage, access, correction, security, and worker-data governance.

- National Institute of Standards and Technology, *Privacy Framework*.
  [Official site](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework).

  This voluntary framework provides a structure for identifying,
  governing, controlling, communicating, and protecting privacy risk. It does
  not replace applicable law or workplace agreements.

## Algorithms in employment

- Raghavan, M., Barocas, S., Kleinberg, J., and Levy, K. (2020), "Mitigating
  Bias in Algorithmic Hiring: Evaluating Claims and Practices," in
  *Proceedings of the 2020 Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and
  Transparency*, 469-481.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3351095.3372828).

  The study examines vendor practices and finds important gaps between bias
  mitigation claims, definitions, evidence, and actual hiring contexts.

- Kellogg, K. C., Valentine, M. A., and Christin, A. (2020), "Algorithms at
  Work: The New Contested Terrain of Control," *Academy of Management Annals*,
  14(1), 366-410.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/annals.2018.0174).

  This review describes how algorithmic systems can expand direction,
  evaluation, and discipline while producing new forms of contestation. LIFT
  uses it as adjacent support for prohibiting hidden automated personnel
  scoring.
