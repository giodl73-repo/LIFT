# Module 02: Advocacy in Action

Status: draft

## Purpose

This module turns advocacy from a vague intention into observable management
practice.

Advocacy is:

> the accurate movement of evidence, credit, context, and opportunity through
> an organization.

It is not flattery, personal branding, favoritism, or guaranteed advancement.

## The advocacy sequence

```text
See
  -> Name
  -> Connect
  -> Open
  -> Back
  -> Transfer
```

The sequence is not a mandatory pipeline. It is a diagnostic for identifying
where advocacy currently breaks.

### 1. See the contribution

Understand:

- what the person actually did;
- which outcome or learning changed;
- who else contributed;
- what evidence supports the account;
- where uncertainty or failure remains.

Advocacy begins with accurate management. A manager who does not understand the
work cannot represent it responsibly.

### 2. Name credit accurately

Connect specific people to specific contributions in appropriate contexts.

Useful attribution:

- names the contribution rather than using generic praise;
- preserves shared and cross-team credit;
- distinguishes leadership, execution, enablement, and support;
- avoids claiming an outcome the evidence does not support;
- corrects material omissions and misattribution.

Naming everyone for everything makes credit meaningless. Naming only the most
visible person erases the system that produced the result.

### 3. Connect contribution to context

Explain why the work matters to the listener:

- which customer, mission, operational, learning, or organizational need it
  serves;
- which constraint or decision it changes;
- how the contribution fits broader work;
- what remains uncertain.

Context relay should translate without inflating. Do not turn a local task into
an enterprise claim merely because a senior audience is present.

### 4. Open an opportunity

Advocacy may create:

- a stretch assignment;
- a presentation or decision role;
- an introduction;
- broader ownership;
- access to information or a network;
- consideration for a role, award, or development path.

An opportunity is not automatically a benefit. Check:

- whether the person wants it;
- whether expectations and decision rights are clear;
- whether support and resources are present;
- whether the opportunity adds growth or merely extra labor;
- who repeatedly receives or misses similar opportunities.

### 5. Back an accurate claim

Sponsorship becomes visible when a manager or leader supports an evidence-based
claim under scrutiny, scarcity, disagreement, or uncertainty.

Backing can include:

- recommending someone for meaningful scope;
- explaining their contribution in a contested decision;
- correcting an inaccurate narrative;
- protecting agreed ownership from arbitrary removal;
- maintaining credit when budgets, attention, or roles become scarce.

Backing is not shielding someone from feedback, consequences, or legitimate
competition. The claim must remain accurate when challenged.

### 6. Transfer access

Good advocacy reduces permanent dependence on the advocate.

Transfer access by:

- creating direct working relationships where appropriate;
- letting the contributor present or decide within their scope;
- ensuring documentation preserves evidence after organizational change;
- teaching the person how organizational context and decisions work;
- building more than one informed sponsorship path.

The goal is not to remain the only person who can explain another person's
value.

## The advocacy floor

Every team member deserves:

1. accurate credit;
2. clear expectations and candid feedback;
3. fair consideration for relevant opportunities;
4. correction of material misrepresentation;
5. a reasonable explanation when an opportunity goes elsewhere.

This floor is not limited to people currently considered promotion-ready.

## Sponsorship as a bounded portfolio

Managers have finite attention, influence, information, and opportunity.
LIFT treats that constraint as a management-design problem, not permission to
quietly choose favorites.

When sponsorship demand exceeds capacity:

- make selection criteria explicit;
- distinguish readiness, development need, and opportunity fit;
- preserve the advocacy floor for everyone;
- rotate scarce developmental opportunities when reasonable;
- ask other qualified leaders to sponsor or mentor;
- create direct evidence paths;
- inspect who repeatedly receives and misses access;
- explain decisions without disclosing another person's private information.

LIFT does not define a universal maximum number of people a manager can
sponsor. Team shape, opportunity size, manager authority, time, and available
networks vary.

## Informal and formal advocacy

Informal advocacy moves context during ordinary decisions. Formal records
preserve evidence, create accountability, and survive memory or leadership
change.

Neither is universally superior.

| Informal channel | Formal channel |
|---|---|
| timely context in a live decision | durable evidence and agreed expectations |
| introductions and opportunity creation | documented contribution and feedback |
| immediate correction of misattribution | reviewable decision rationale |
| relationship and network transfer | continuity after organizational change |

Strong practice uses both without inventing evidence in either.

## Fair-opportunity review

Before assigning a consequential opportunity, ask:

```text
What capability or development need does this opportunity serve?
What criteria are being applied?
Who was considered?
Who has received similar opportunities recently?
Who has repeatedly performed essential but less visible work?
Does the person want this opportunity?
What support makes success plausible?
How will contribution and shared credit be recorded?
```

The review is not a quota or a promise that every assignment rotates evenly.
It exposes repeated patterns that informal judgment can hide.

## Advocacy with consent

People may have legitimate reasons to decline visibility, travel, public
recognition, expanded scope, or a role change.

When practical:

- ask before nominating or exposing someone;
- explain the likely audience, cost, and expectations;
- provide an opt-out without retaliation;
- separate declining one opportunity from declining growth;
- avoid sharing personal circumstances as part of an advocacy story.

Consent may not be possible before every routine credit statement. Accuracy,
privacy, and respect still apply.

## Advocacy after a miss

A manager can advocate honestly when work underperforms:

```text
The predicted result did not occur.
The person produced this valid evidence or learning.
These execution and system conditions mattered.
This is what they own.
This is what the organization should change.
This is the next bounded opportunity they are prepared to take.
```

Do not erase failure, assign all blame to the system, or allow one miss to
erase unrelated contribution.

## Recipient view

Possible signs of advocacy include:

- your contribution remains accurate when retold;
- decision-makers connect your name to specific work;
- opportunities arrive with clear reasons;
- your manager can describe where and how they represented the work;
- you gain direct context and relationships rather than only secondhand praise;
- mistakes and shared credit remain visible.

These are evidence items, not proof that every desired outcome will follow.

## Manager self-review

For each person over a meaningful period, ask:

```text
What contribution can I describe specifically?
Where did I name credit accurately?
Which broader context did I connect?
Which opportunity did I open or fairly decline?
Which accurate claim did I back?
Which access path became less dependent on me?
What feedback or expectation did I leave unclear?
```

Review patterns across the team. Do not turn the answers into a secret employee
score.

## What this module does not claim

- One advocacy behavior universally produces the greatest return.
- Saying a person's name a specific number of times changes promotion odds.
- Managers should actively sponsor only a fixed number of people.
- Formal documentation is less important than informal advocacy.
- Advocacy can compensate for unsupported performance claims.
- Stretch work is beneficial without consent, support, or fair allocation.
- A manager's network determines a person's career outcome.
- Equal treatment means identical opportunities regardless of need or fit.
